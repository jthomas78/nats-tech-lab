Four words used on every slide that follows

Instance, cluster, gateway, leaf

The shape below is the one we would actually deploy: two AWS regions, three availability zones each, one NATS server per zone. Three zones is why a cluster is three servers — lose a zone and two of three still holds a majority.

1 · NATS instance

One server process. It holds data and answers clients. Here, one per availability zone.

2 · Cluster

Three or more instances that vote together, inside one region. This is the unit of availability, and the only thing three servers buys you.

3 · Gateway

Joins two clusters and routes messages between them. It does not copy data — and it fuses both sides into one JetStream. See slide 06.

4 · Leaf node

A server that borrows a hub over one outbound link. It may run its own JetStream, which is the only place jetstream.domain belongs. See slide 07.

Read the dotted band

The account is drawn across both regions, not inside one. Region and cell are places. Tenant and account are not, and a drawing that nests them inside a region is the mistake ADR-056 was written to stop.

The three-instance cluster

Three servers is not three copies

A cluster is three instances because RAFT needs a majority to elect a leader, and the majority of three is two — so one instance can be lost. That protects the cluster. It does not protect your stream. Replication is a separate, per-stream choice, and its default is one.

The trap
  • The default is 1, and nothing warns you. A three-server cluster with an R1 stream survives nothing.
  • Replicas never cross a region. --replicas 3 means three copies in za — not one in each region.
  • R3 is slower and uses three times the disk. Choose it on purpose, per stream.
What --replicas 3 actually copies
Copied 3 times, inside za
  • Stream messages — the log itself.
  • Stream config — subjects, limits, retention.
  • Consumer position and acks — its own vote group, which inherits 3 from the stream.
  • KV bucket values — a KV bucket is a stream.
  • Object store blobs — also a stream.
Handled somewhere else
  • The fact that the stream exists. That lives in the meta group, on every server in the system — not in the replica set.
Not copied at all
  • Anything in au. A gateway routes; it does not copy. That needs a mirror.
  • Ephemeral consumers — R1, in memory, and they die with their server.
Recommendation

Start at three instances, one cluster. Set Replicas: 3 explicitly on every stream and KV bucket that matters. Make it a reviewed default, not a habit.

Two islands never freeze, and never agree

The baseline. Two clusters of three, with nothing joining them. Each region runs its own JetStream and its own vote. This is the only shape where a region going dark changes nothing at all for the other one — and the only shape that shares nothing whatsoever.

What it gives you
  • Two separate meta groups, of three servers each. A majority of three is two, so each region tolerates losing one server. T1a, T1b, T1c
  • ZA goes dark and AU does not notice. AU keeps its meta leader, creates a brand-new 3-replica stream, and its meta group size is unchanged at 3. T1g, T1h, T1i
  • No WAN on any read or write. Nothing to fail between the regions, because there is nothing between them.
What it costs you
  • Two streams can carry the same name and be unrelated. AU asks for ODOMETER, gets it, and it is a second real stream. No error, because there is no shared namespace to collide in. T1d, T1e
  • Nothing crosses. One publish in ZA: ZA holds 1 message, AU holds 0. T1f
  • Every cross-region copy is an application you have to write, run and monitor yourself.

The most expensive thing on these slides

A gateway makes one JetStream, not two

This is the finding that changes a design. A gateway does not join two JetStream systems — it fuses them into one supercluster with one meta group and one leader election. The account wall still holds for data. It does not hold for availability.

Read the two drawings together

Nothing about the servers changed. Only the link did — and with it, the arithmetic. Six votes become three, three is not four, and every JetStream change stops in both regions at once.

One namespace, one copy, and one shared fate

The previous slide showed the arithmetic. These are the consequences you will actually meet: losing a whole region is the same sum as losing the link, names collide, data lives in exactly one place, an uneven split behaves differently — and some things carry on regardless.

If a whole region dies, not just the link
  • The meta-leader's location does not matter. Lose any 3 of 6 and you are under 4. A10
  • AU still publishes into the streams it holds. It cannot create anything new. A11, A12
  • ZA's streams are not in AU. A gateway routes; it never copies. A8
  • For 6 seconds /jsz still named the dead meta-leader. A10a
One namespace means one name
  • AU asks for a stream ZA already owns → 10058 stream name already in use
  • Overlapping subjects → 10065 subjects overlap
  • So inside one account a single publish can never be stored twice.
…and one copy, in one place
  • AU's client keeps working, because a stream is reachable from either side of a gateway.
  • But stream info from AU reports cluster za. AU holds nothing.
  • AU pays the WAN on every read and goes dark for that stream when ZA is lost.
Uneven regions behave differently
  • A 3 / 3 split freezes both sides.
  • A 3 / 2 split does not — the side holding 3 of 5 keeps its leader.
  • Which side survives is arithmetic, so design the count deliberately.
What a 3/3 split freezes
  • stream add / rm / edit → 10008 JetStream system temporarily unavailable
  • Every account at the same moment. Measured with 3 JetStream accounts live: 1 meta group, not three.
What keeps working
  • Core NATS publish and subscribe.
  • Reads from a stream that already exists.
  • Writes into a stream that already exists — measured 10 → 15 messages, all acked.
The decision

If both regions must accept a JetStream change while the other is dark, a plain two-region gateway cannot do it. Add a vote outside both regions, or stop sharing a meta group.

The setting everybody reaches for first

A domain is a leaf tool. A gateway is not a leaf link.

jetstream.domain looks like the fix for the previous slide. It is not. We set it over a gateway, measured it, and it made things quietly worse. Over a leaf link the same setting is real and does exactly what the name promises.

Over a gateway — measured
  • Meta group size still 6. A domain name cannot split a supercluster.
  • One cluster elects, the other reports leader: null forever.
  • Placing a stream on the blind cluster → 10005 no suitable peers for placement
  • The verdict in the report is blunt: does not work.
Over a leaf link — measured
  • 3 distinct meta groups, sizes 3 / 3 / 3. Hub, ZA and AU each own their JetStream.
  • The whole hub can be stopped and both regions still elect, still create R3 streams, still store writes.
  • This is the only shape in the lab that survives the centre disappearing.
The price nobody mentions
  • Double capture is real here. One publish in ZA was stored 1 and 1 — twice, in two systems, with no warning.
  • Nothing replicates by itself. Every cross-region copy is hand-written.
  • A mirror over a leaf needs mirror.external.api → $JS.au.API. Without it: 0 messages copied, and the stream reports healthy.
The rule

A JetStream domain is a leaf-link tool. Setting it over a gateway is not a smaller version of the same thing — it is a misconfiguration that reports success.

Buy a vote that sits outside both regions

T2 freezes because 3 of 6 is not a majority. Both fixes put more voters in a third site, so a surviving region plus that site still adds up. T3 adds one node, T4 adds three, and the difference is slack.

T3 — one arbiter node · 7 servers, majority 4
  • One meta group across all three sites. C1, C2
  • Either region can go dark and the survivors elect a leader and still create a new 3-replica stream. C3–C6
  • Zero slack. 4 of 7 is exactly the majority. One more node after the region loss and it freezes. C12
T4 — a three-node arbiter site · 9 servers, majority 5
  • Still one meta group, size 9. F1, F2
  • A region dark leaves 6 of 9 — a leader is elected and writes continue. F3, F4
  • One node of slack. A region plus one arbiter node leaves 5 of 9, exactly the majority, and it still works. At 4 of 9 the leader is NONE. F5, F6, F7, F11
  • Even with no majority, publishing into an existing stream still works. Only changes stop. F8, F9
The arbiter trap — it will quietly host your data
  • An explicit --cluster arb R1 stream lands on the arbiter, and a client dialling the arbiter's own port creates streams there too. C8, C10
  • Asking for R3 on a one-node site fails with 10005. C9
  • So: keep clients off the arbiter's client port, or fence it with placement. C11
One number worth remembering

After the majority was gone, /jsz went on naming a meta leader for 40 seconds. F7a

And once a new leader was named, it accepted a change immediately — 0 seconds. F6a

Late in one direction, early in the other. Neither edge survives a single reading.

A whole region dies — and the answer flips
  • ZA dies holding the meta-leader. T3 keeps 4 of 7, T4 keeps 6 of 9 — still a majority, so the survivors elect one and AU creates new streams. C5a, C6, F4
  • The leader's address never mattered. Only the count does.
  • ZA's data is still gone. A gateway routes, it never copies.
  • On slide 06 the same loss leaves 3 of 6 and freezes both. That is what the third site buys. A10
Choose an arbiter when

Both regions must accept JetStream changes while the other is dark, and you can run a third site. Take T4, not T3 — T3 survives the region loss with nothing left over, and a single extra node failure then freezes everything.

Independence you can have, at the price of copying by hand

A leaf link is the other way out of T2. Each region keeps its own JetStream and its own vote, so the hub can vanish entirely and both regions carry on — but nothing then replicates by itself.

T5 — hub and leaf · three separate JetStream systems
  • Three meta groups, sizes 3 / 3 / 3. Each has its own quorum of two. D1, D2
  • The whole hub can vanish. Both regions keep a leader, both create new 3-replica streams, and AU keeps storing its own publishes. D10–D14
  • Both regions may own a stream called ODOMETER with no 10058, because there is no shared namespace. D3, D4
T5 — what it costs
  • Double capture is real here. ONE publish in ZA was stored in ZA and in AU — 1 / 1 — and two same-named shadow streams lived at once. D5, D6
  • Nothing replicates by itself. Every cross-region copy is hand-written, and the panel below is why that is harder than it sounds. D15
So how does data get from one leaf to the other? You write it by hand.
  • A leaf link carries messages, not the catalogue — ZA cannot see AU's streams at all. D1
  • What works is a mirror naming the remote API: external.api $JS.au.API → 2 copied. D8
  • Leave that line out and you get silence. 0 messages, no error, no warning, stream healthy. D7, D7a
  • Worse: a name-only mirror silently copies the local stream of that name. D9, D9a

Rule: always write external.api. A green mirror is not evidence that anything was copied.

Choose a leaf link when

Regional independence matters more than one namespace — and budget for the copiers. Wherever you do share, share it deliberately: name the remote API, or use the account wall on the next slide. Never by giving two streams the same name and hoping.

The one safe way to copy across a wall

E is not T5. It has two clusters and a gateway, and no leaf nodes at all. It answers a different question: if two accounts must share one subject, how do you do it so nobody can ever confuse the copy with the original?

What it does
  • ZA publishes once. The owning account stored it, and the importing account LB_AU stored it too, across the gateway. E1, E2
  • It arrives renamed. LB_AU's raw-subject stream took none of it — the importing side prefixes the subject. E3
  • So the deliberate copy and an accidental one can never be confused. E3a
  • When LB_AU publishes the same subject itself, ZA's stream is unchanged. Traffic only moves the way you declared. E4, E5
The account wall still holds
  • LB_AU asking for stream info on ZA's ODOMETER fails. E6
  • And LB_AU may create its own stream called ODOMETER with no 10058. E7
  • They are two real, separate streams, in za and au. E8
  • Compare slide 06: one account over a gateway is one namespace, and that name collides.
Share this way

Explicit, one direction, and renamed on arrival — the only sharing measured in this demo that a reader cannot misread. E9 Wherever two tenants or two regions must see the same event, put an account wall between them and export across it. Do not share by giving two streams the same name and hoping.

One server → one cluster → two regions · and the one step that is not a step

Growing is cheap until the last move

Every other slide shows a shape. This one shows the order. Three of the moves are small and ordinary. The last one is not a move at all — it is a cutover, and the lab measured why.

The blue arrows are design, not measurement — demo 03 built every shape from nothing and never ran a migration. The red wall is measured: G24.
Steps 1 to 3 — ordinary
  • One server is a starting point, not a design. It has no quorum and no HA.
  • Three servers in one cluster is the first real shape: majority 2, one node may die.
  • Going from one server to a cluster, every member needs at least one route — the seed included. A routeless seed with JetStream on refuses to start.
  • Step 3 is where the whole decision lives — and it is a fork, not a line.
The wall — and this part is measured
  • T6 put a gateway and a hub leaf link on the same servers. Result: two JetStream systems, not three. G1, G2
  • The gateway still fuses both regions into one group of 6, and both name the same meta leader. G3, G15
  • 10058 comes back, and T5's double capture collapses to one stored copy read through two handles. G4, G7, G7a
  • A dark region still freezes every create with 10008, and a healthy hub cannot lend a vote. G11a, G14a
So decide on day one
  • If a region must survive alone, go 1 → 3 → hub and leaf. Do not stop at a gateway on the way.
  • If one namespace is the point, the gateway is the simpler shape — then buy the vote back with a three-node arbiter site. One node of slack, not zero.
  • Changing your mind later means a restart of both regions and a stream that must be split in two.
Pick the end shape first

There is no gradual path from a gateway to regional isolation. Adding the hub link early buys nothing — the gateway decides for as long as it exists. G24 The cheap moves are 1 → 3 → a second region; the expensive move is changing which kind of second region you chose.

Where to start — and stay
  • One cluster of three. It survives one loss, and it is the whole of what most of the platform needs.
  • Replicas: 3 set explicitly on every stream and KV bucket that matters.
  • Accounts as the tenant wall from day one. Retrofitting that boundary later is the expensive move.
What we have not measured yet
  • The latency cost of a cross-region read. Placement is measured; latency is not.
  • Mirror lag and bandwidth in either shape.
  • A hub that holds data of its own, rather than only relaying.
  • Leaf reconnect with more than two regions.
The next question

Every shape here is available today and we can pick one on the evidence. What we cannot yet price is the wire: what a cross-border byte costs in latency and in bandwidth.

That is the next demo, and it is the one that decides whether a gateway between regions is affordable at all.

In one line

Three nodes get you further than anyone expects. The moment a second region appears, the choice is shared namespace or independent availability — and you cannot have both.

Streams, consumers, KV and the cost of replay

A KV bucket is a stream, and a consumer lives where its stream lives

Placement is not a separate subject from clustering — it is the same subject seen from inside one cluster. And once state is rebuilt from a log, the length of that log becomes a latency number you have to manage.

The CQRS shape measured in demo 04
command→ rehydrate the aggregate→ check the rule→ append
stream ODOMETER→ write consumer→ KV odometer-write — snapshot
stream ODOMETER→ read consumer→ KV odometer-read — read model

The log is the only source of truth. Delete both KV buckets and everything comes back. Delete the stream and nothing does.

Rehydration — 10 001 events on one vehicle

Replay is linear in the length of the log. The snapshot read is one KV get and does not care how long the log is. Take the log to 100k events and the left-hand bar grows tenfold while the right-hand one does not move.

Placement — the rules we measured
  • Everything lands where the account's first request landed, unless you name a cluster with --cluster.
  • A KV bucket follows the same rule, because a bucket is a stream.
  • A durable pull consumer created from AU for a ZA-held stream reported cluster za. AU held no part of it and paid the WAN on every pull.
Two settings that fail silently
  • LimitsPolicy, not InterestPolicy, on any stream you intend to replay. InterestPolicy discards a message once every consumer has acked it, and the replay then returns nothing — with no error.
  • The snapshot is always stale. Rehydration must read the snapshot and then replay the tail. Measured mid-catch-up: 2 900 events of tail in 12 ms.
Storage — the standing rule
  • A count is never shown without its bytes. 100 000 000 events sounds reasonable right up to the moment you learn it is 7.6 GB.
  • R3 triples that figure, per stream, per region.
  • Seeding one million events into the bench fixture takes about 2.2 seconds — the log grows faster than anyone plans for.
Recommendation

Treat snapshotting as a requirement, not an optimisation, on any aggregate that will take more than a few thousand events. And state a retention policy on day one, in bytes.

Findings this lab added

Five failures that report success

These are not in the documentation. Each one was found by a script that expected a specific answer and got a different one. Every one of them looks healthy from the outside — which is exactly why they are on a slide.

01 · The monitoring endpoint lies while it is stale

After the majority of a supercluster was frozen, /jsz kept naming a meta leader for roughly a minute. Read it too early and a dead supercluster looks perfectly healthy.

In that window the client does not get a clean refusal either — it hangs and times out. The clean 10008 only appears once the old leader has aged out.

02 · A new leader is named before it can take a change

After an election, /jsz names the new leader seconds before that leader will accept a create. Ask immediately and the call is refused — on a supercluster that is in fact healthy.

So neither edge of a failover can be trusted from a single reading. Health checks must retry and record how long they waited, not sleep a magic number.

03 · A name-only mirror can copy the WRONG stream

A mirror with no external.api does not fail when the name it wants only exists in the other region — it just sits at zero. Worse: if a stream of the same name also exists locally, the mirror copies that one, reports healthy, and looks like it is working.

Proved by giving the two streams different message counts. No error, no warning, wrong data.

04 · Which region goes blind is a coin toss

Two runs of the same configuration, minutes apart, nothing changed — and the blind side swapped.

A team that tests this shape once will write down the wrong region's name. Our check deliberately refuses to name a side.

05 · A test that does not test reads exactly like a result

Not a NATS fact — a rig fact, and the most important one. An early version of the harness recorded the wrong process id, so kill -STOP hit nothing and the “frozen” region stayed fully alive. The lab reported healthy behaviour and it looked like a finding. Every freeze in these scripts now proves itself first, by checking the monitor port has really gone dark.

Two numbers we retracted
  • A cross-region “double capture” of 50 km was a replay bug — a durable consumer was being deleted on every run.
  • A rehydration speed-up of 600× was 99% consumer bookkeeping, not log reading. The real figure is 23×.
  • A lab whose numbers only ever improve is not measuring.
Operational consequence

Do not build alerting, failover automation or a runbook on a single read of /jsz. Every one of these failures passes a naive health check.
